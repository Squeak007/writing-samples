# Portfolio Sample 2 — Newsletter Issue (Ghostwritten for a business coach)

*Target audience:* Email list of a hypothetical business coach's audience (small business owners, service providers).
*Target length:* ~650 words
*Tone:* Warm, confident, slightly conversational, one real idea per issue.

---

*Subject:* The question I ask every stuck client (and almost no one asks themselves)

Last week I was on a call with a client who runs a small design agency. Six years in, five employees, revenue around $800K. She was frustrated. "We're busy all the time," she said, "but I feel like we're running in place."

I asked her the question I ask every client who feels stuck. It is one of the most useful questions I know, and almost no one asks it on their own.

*"What would you have to stop doing to grow?"*

She looked at me like I was speaking Dutch.

Most of us, when we feel stuck, ask ourselves the opposite question. What should I add? What should I start? What new service, new channel, new hire, new tool, new habit? We treat growth as additive. More of the right things equals more results.

But growth is almost always subtractive first. The things you need to stop doing are the things that have been quietly filling the space the new work would need to occupy. And the catch is that those things are usually things you are already good at. Things that already produce some result. Things it would feel irresponsible to drop.

My client, for example, was doing all of the following personally:

- Running every client intake call
- Writing every project proposal
- Final-reviewing every deliverable before send
- Managing the agency's social media presence
- Posting weekly on LinkedIn to generate leads

Each of these was a reasonable thing for a founder to do. Each produced some result. And together, they were consuming about 35 hours a week — leaving her five hours a week to actually think about the agency as a business, which is what the next phase of growth required.

The question wasn't "What should I add?" It was "What is already here that I need to let go of?"

*The hard part is the attachment.*

We stay attached to the things we got good at. We tell ourselves that no one else can do them as well, which is usually both true (nobody will do them exactly the same way) and irrelevant (nobody has to). We tell ourselves that if we hand them off, something will break, which is sometimes true in the short run and almost never true in the long run.

I don't know what you'd have to stop doing to grow. I don't know your business. But I know the shape of the answer. It's probably something you've been doing for a while. It's probably something you are known for being good at. It's probably something that produces real results today, which is exactly why you haven't considered dropping it.

If you want a drill for yourself this week, try this:

1. List the top five things you spent time on this week.
2. For each, ask: *Does this thing make sense for me specifically to do, or does it make sense for someone to do and I'm the easiest default?*
3. The ones where you're the easiest default — not the best person, just the easiest — are candidates to subtract.

You will not act on this immediately, and that's fine. Awareness comes before action. But the next time you catch yourself asking "what should I start doing," pause for a second and ask the harder question. It changes the shape of the answer.

Talk soon,

[Client first name]

P.S. — My client decided to stop doing final-review on deliverables (she promoted her lead designer to a quality role) and to stop writing proposals (she templated them and had her ops person adapt). Three months later, revenue was up 18% and she was working four fewer hours a day. Subtracting works.
